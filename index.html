Current assessment

The evidence is converging faster than any single forecast.

No public result shows that a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists today. The bigger story · resource-estimate compression

The target is moving — but the estimates are not like-for-like

The cleanest apples-to-apples result is Gidney's RSA-2048 estimate: about 20 million noisy qubits in 2019 to fewer than 1 million in 2025 under the same stated hardware assumptions. A separate 2026 neutral-atom study reaches cryptographically relevant Shor-scale workloads with as few as 10,000 reconfigurable atomic qubits, but uses a different architecture, error-correction design and runtime trade-off. Read these together as evidence that algorithms, error correction and hardware-aware co-design are all reducing the resources thought necessary for cryptanalysis.

Representative published physical-qubit resource estimates (log scale; not like-for-like)
Representative estimates: RSA-2048 20 million in 2019; RSA-2048 under 1 million in 2025 under the same stated assumptions; and a separate 2026 neutral-atom study with a roughly 10,000-qubit floor for cryptographically relevant Shor-scale workloads.

Do not read the three points as one continuous RSA curve. The 2019 and 2025 RSA estimates are directly comparable because Gidney states the same hardware assumptions. The 2026 Cain et al. result is architecture-specific neutral-atom/QEC co-design and trades space against much longer runtime. The defensible conclusion is resource compression across multiple technical fronts, not “RSA now needs 10,000 qubits.” Full sources below.

Why this matters alongside the survey: an annual opinion poll of 26 people is a sentiment indicator, while peer-reviewed experiments and resource analyses provide different evidence. Several important 2026 results appeared after the 2025 survey period, so they should be tracked separately rather than retroactively folded into the survey number.

Timeline

The evidence landscape behind the timeline

Since 2022, two technical directions have mattered simultaneously: published cryptanalytic resource estimates have fallen, while hardware and quantum-error-correction experiments have improved. Standards and national migration programmes have also moved from preparation toward implementation. These streams should be read together, but not treated as the same kind of evidence.

◆ What the 2025 report discusses — and what it does not prove

The 2025 Quantum Threat Timeline Report discusses recent hardware and cryptanalytic-resource developments, including Google Willow and lower resource estimates, as part of the context for an accelerating threat assessment. That does not establish that any single development caused the survey probabilities to move. Events below are therefore labelled by evidence type and timing rather than presented as statistically proven drivers.

2022
Baseline Survey

The quiet starting point

The 2022 survey provides a useful baseline for expert opinion: the 10-year CRQC probability was still below the levels reported in later editions. It should be read as an opinion snapshot, not as a statement that quantum error correction or fault-tolerance research was absent at the time.

Mechanism

The value of the 2022 point is comparative. It anchors how expert judgement changed over subsequent editions while the underlying hardware, error-correction and cryptanalytic-resource literature evolved.

The numbers

IBM announced the 433-qubit Osprey processor in 2022, while the GRI survey still placed the 10-year cryptographic threat below the later 2025 range. These are different evidence types and should not be treated as a direct causal relationship.

The honest caveat

Annual survey movement should not be over-interpreted. Respondent pools, probability bins and expert judgement introduce uncertainty, so multi-year direction matters more than a single-year change.

Front 1 · Cryptanalytic resource compression — the target shrinks

These results reduce estimated resources for attacks on RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography. They are technical estimates, not evidence that the required hardware already exists.

2023Aug
Theory Foundational

Regev breaks a 30-year deadlock on Shor's algorithm

Oded Regev published the first fundamental improvement to Shor's factoring algorithm in nearly three decades — a multidimensional variant needing far fewer quantum gates. Subsequent work explored how the approach and related arithmetic improvements could affect concrete quantum resource estimates.

Mechanism

Shor's 1994 algorithm was the benchmark for "how hard is it to break RSA" for 29 years. Regev found a genuinely new formulation — operating in a higher-dimensional space — that cuts the circuit size (gate count). Because every later resource estimate builds on the best available algorithm, a foundational improvement like this cascades into every downstream qubit count. It's the source of the collapse in the chart above.

The numbers

Reduces circuit size by a factor of ~√n for an n-bit integer, at the cost of ~√n separate runs. Follow-on work (Ragavan & Vaikuntanathan, Oct 2023) cut the space overhead back down, addressing its main drawback.

The honest caveat

Regev's algorithm relies on a heuristic number-theoretic assumption (since given a proof under certain conditions), and early implementations were actually slower than Shor's for small numbers. Its value is asymptotic and foundational, not an immediate practical speedup — but it reset the theoretical baseline.

Primary source: Regev, arXiv:2308.06572 (2023) ↗
2025Feb
Theory Report-cited context

The 1,730-qubit result (Chevignard–Fouque–Schrottenloher)

A CRYPTO 2025 paper cut the number of logical qubits needed to factor RSA-2048 to just 1,730 — providing the algorithmic foundation that the Gidney estimate then compiled into physical hardware numbers. The 2025 report names this as a key theoretical advance its experts cited.

Mechanism

The threat timeline isn't only about building bigger machines — it's also about needing smaller ones. Every algorithmic improvement that reduces the qubits required to break RSA pulls Q-Day closer without a single new piece of hardware. This paper attacked the problem from the maths side, using approximate residue arithmetic to shrink the logical-qubit requirement dramatically.

The numbers

Reduced the logical-qubit count for factoring RSA-2048 to ~1,730 — roughly (0.5 + ε)·n logical qubits for an n-bit integer. This is the algorithmic groundwork the subsequent physical-resource estimate was built on.

The honest caveat

Logical qubits are not physical qubits — the earlier version of this algorithm needed an astronomically high gate count (~2×10¹² Toffoli gates), making it impractical until the follow-on work optimized it. It's a foundation, not a turnkey attack.

Primary source: Chevignard, Fouque & Schrottenloher, IACR ePrint 2024/1291 ↗
2025May
Theory Resource estimate

Gidney: RSA-2048 with under 1 million qubits

Craig Gidney’s May 2025 paper reduced his estimated physical-qubit requirement for factoring RSA-2048 from 20 million in 2019 to fewer than 1 million under the same stated hardware assumptions. The result is a major resource-estimate update; it does not demonstrate an RSA-2048 attack.

Mechanism

The significance is that the estimated hardware target moved substantially under a comparable assumption set. The reduction comes from improvements in arithmetic, logical-qubit storage and magic-state handling rather than from a new physical machine.

The numbers

2019 estimate: about 20 million noisy qubits and an eight-hour runtime. 2025 estimate: fewer than 1 million noisy qubits and a runtime of less than one week. Both use a nearest-neighbour grid, 0.1% gate error, 1 μs surface-code cycle and 10 μs reaction time.

The honest caveat

This remains a resource estimate. No existing machine can execute the attack, and the result should not be converted into a CRQC arrival date. It is best used as evidence that cryptanalytic resource requirements can change materially as algorithms and fault-tolerance techniques improve.

Primary source: Gidney, arXiv:2505.15917 (2025) ↗
2026Mar
Theory Hardware-specific

Neutral-atom co-design pushes Shor-scale estimates toward ~10,000 qubits

A Caltech/Oratomic/UC Berkeley team (Cain, Preskill, Bluvstein et al.) reported that cryptographically relevant Shor-scale computation could be possible with as few as ~10,000 reconfigurable atomic qubits by co-designing algorithms, high-rate quantum error correction and neutral-atom hardware. This is a different architecture and runtime regime from Gidney's RSA estimate, so it should not be plotted as a direct continuation of the same curve.

Mechanism

This is where algorithm and hardware modality meet. qLDPC codes need long-range connections between qubits — something superconducting chips struggle with, but neutral atoms do naturally because they can be physically moved. Matching the code to the hardware slashes the requirement again. It also means watching only one hardware type (e.g. superconducting) gives a false sense of how far away the threat is.

The numbers

The paper's abstract gives an overall floor of about 10,000 reconfigurable atomic qubits for cryptographically relevant Shor-scale workloads. For P-256 it gives an example of about 26,000 physical qubits and a runtime of a few days; it says RSA-2048 would take one to two orders of magnitude longer. Those figures are architecture- and assumption-dependent.

The honest caveat

These are aggressive, assumption-heavy resource estimates with very long runtimes, and no neutral-atom machine is close to 10,000 error-corrected qubits today. But a slow code-breaker doesn't need to be fast — for "harvest now, decrypt later," patience is enough.

Primary source: Cain et al., arXiv:2603.28627 (2026) ↗
2026Mar
Theory Context

Google: P-256 ECDLP below 1,200 logical qubits in a 2026 resource study

Google researchers published new resource estimates for the 256-bit elliptic-curve discrete-log problem used in many modern TLS, SSH, code-signing and cryptocurrency deployments. Their study gives circuits below 1,200 logical qubits and models execution on a superconducting architecture using fewer than half a million physical qubits.

Mechanism

The GRI survey uses RSA-2048 as its benchmark, but many deployed systems rely on elliptic-curve public-key cryptography. Tracking ECDLP resource estimates therefore gives a complementary view of quantum risk; organisations should inventory the algorithms they actually use rather than infer their exposure from RSA alone.

The numbers

The 2026 whitepaper reports either <1,200 logical qubits with <90 million Toffoli gates, or <1,450 logical qubits with <70 million Toffoli gates. Under its model of a superconducting architecture with 10−3 physical error rates and planar connectivity, the circuits can run in minutes using fewer than 500,000 physical qubits.

The honest caveat

Still a resource estimate, not a demonstration. But it reframes the target: migration plans that track only RSA timelines may underestimate the relevance of elliptic-curve cryptography to the threat surface.

Primary source: Google Research / arXiv:2603.28846 (2026) ↗

Front 2 · The hardware & policy front — the machine gets closer

Real processors, error-correction milestones, national programs, and the standards/deadlines that turn all of it into an obligation.

2023Dec
Hardware pivot Context

From more qubits to better qubits

Investment described as skyrocketing. IBM ships its 1,121-qubit Condor but publicly pivots toward modular, error-corrected designs — conceding that raw scaling alone won't reach fault tolerance. 10-year survey estimate: 17–31%.

Mechanism

The tell was in one processor pair. IBM's giant 1,121-qubit Condor performed no better than the previous 433-qubit chip — proof that just adding qubits had hit diminishing returns. The smaller Heron, with far lower error rates, was the real signal: the industry pivoted from "biggest" to "cleanest."

The numbers

Condor: 1,121 qubits but roughly Osprey-level (433-qubit) performance. Heron: only 133 qubits, but error rates cut to about a third of the prior generation. 10-year survey estimate rose to 17–31%.

The honest caveat

Lower error rates on a small chip are not the same as error correction. Heron made qubits better; it did not yet make them self-correcting. That threshold wasn't crossed until Willow the following year. Note: this is landscape context — the survey does not name Condor as a driver.

Source: IBM Quantum roadmap ↗
2024Aug
Policy Context

NIST finalizes the PQC standards

FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), 204 (ML-DSA) and 205 (SLH-DSA) were published in August 2024, giving organisations final NIST standards for post-quantum key establishment and digital signatures. This turns algorithm selection into an implementation and migration problem, while actual obligations still depend on sector, policy and jurisdiction.

Mechanism

Standardisation removes a major blocker: organisations can now design migration programmes around named algorithms and parameter sets. It does not mean every system is immediately required to migrate, nor does it guarantee that implementations and protocol integrations are secure.

The numbers

FIPS 203, 204 and 205 were finalised on 13 Aug 2024. HQC was selected in March 2025 as an additional KEM for future standardisation. NIST IR 8547 remains an Initial Public Draft as of this fact-check and should not be presented as a final binding transition rule.

The honest caveat

Safe migration requires more than a standard: implementations, protocol integration, key and secret lifecycle, configuration, testing and assurance all remain separate engineering risks. Policy obligations should be attributed to the specific law, executive order, security policy or sector rule that creates them.

Primary source: NIST FIPS 203/204/205, Federal Register (2024) ↗
2024Dec
Hardware Report-cited context

Google Willow — below threshold

Google reported below-threshold surface-code quantum memories on its Willow processors: logical error decreased as code distance increased, a key condition for scalable quantum error correction.

Mechanism

Below-threshold operation means increasing the size of the error-correcting code suppresses logical error rather than amplifying it. That behaviour is foundational for large fault-tolerant systems, but it is still several engineering steps removed from cryptanalytic computation.

The numbers

On the 105-qubit Willow processor, the distance-7 surface-code memory used 101 qubits and reported a logical error of 0.143% per cycle with an error-suppression factor Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02. The logical memory lifetime exceeded its best constituent physical qubit by about 2.4×.

The honest caveat

This was an error-corrected memory experiment, not an RSA or ECC attack and not a general-purpose fault-tolerant computer. It demonstrates an important scaling property, while large-scale computation still requires much larger systems, logical operations, decoding, stability and system integration.

Primary source: Acharya et al., Nature (2024) ↗
2025Mar
Hardware Geopolitical

China's Zuchongzhi 3.0 — the two-horse race is real

A USTC team (Pan Jianwei et al.) unveiled a 105-qubit superconducting processor claiming quantum advantage on random-circuit sampling far beyond classical reach. The 2025 GRI report itself flags China as the most likely challenger within five years — and notes zero survey respondents are based there.

Mechanism

The threat timeline isn't only technical — it's geopolitical. A cryptographically relevant machine built in secret by a state actor wouldn't be announced; the report explicitly warns covert programs could put the true timeline 2+ years ahead of open publications. Visible parity between US and Chinese hardware is a proxy for how fast the invisible race may be moving.

The numbers

105 qubits, 182 couplers. Gate fidelities: 99.90% single-qubit, 99.62% two-qubit (PRL 134, 090601). Claimed a random-circuit-sampling task that would take the Frontier supercomputer ~5.9 billion years. Note: this is a sampling benchmark, not a code-breaking demonstration.

The honest caveat

Random-circuit sampling is a contrived benchmark with no cryptographic relevance, and classical algorithms sometimes claw back the "advantage" later. This shows national capability and pace, not proximity to breaking RSA.

Primary source: Phys. Rev. Lett. 134, 090601 (2025) ↗
2025Nov
Hardware Different modality

Quantinuum Helios — trapped ions hit 48 logical qubits

Quantinuum's 98-physical-qubit trapped-ion Helios system reported 99.921% two-qubit gate fidelity and demonstrations including 48 error-corrected logical qubits at a 2:1 physical-to-logical encoding rate. A reminder that fault-tolerance progress must be tracked across multiple hardware modalities, not only superconducting systems.

Mechanism

Helios uses trapped ions with high connectivity and high physical-gate fidelity, and Quantinuum demonstrated high-rate logical encodings using concatenated codes. The 2:1 result is a specific code-and-workload demonstration; it should not be read as a universal physical-to-logical conversion ratio for arbitrary fault-tolerant computation.

The numbers

Quantinuum reports 98 physical qubits at 99.921% two-qubit gate fidelity and 99.9975% one-qubit gate fidelity. Helios demonstrations include 94 error-detected logical qubits in a GHZ state, 50 error-detected logical qubits in a magnetism simulation, and 48 error-corrected logical qubits with better-than-physical performance.

The honest caveat

48 logical qubits is worlds away from the thousands needed for RSA, and trapped-ion clock speeds are slower than superconducting. High fidelity and low overhead are real advantages, but scale remains the open question for every platform.

Source: Quantinuum roadmap & Helios ↗
2024Sep
Error correction Overhead cut

Magic state cultivation — a proposed route to lower fault-tolerant overhead

Gidney, Shutty & Jones proposed “magic state cultivation,” a protocol designed to produce the non-Clifford resource states needed for universal fault-tolerant computation with physical-gate costs comparable to a lattice-surgery CNOT of similar reliability. It targets one of the expensive components in fault-tolerant resource budgets.

Mechanism

Error-corrected machines can do "easy" (Clifford) gates cheaply, but universal computation needs "magic states," historically made by distillation — enormous factories consuming huge numbers of physical qubits. Cultivation grows them directly inside a logical qubit, potentially eliminating whole distillation rounds. Every resource estimate above (Gidney's <1M, the neutral-atom 10K) quietly depends on advances like this to be credible.

The numbers

The cited 2024 paper is a protocol-and-simulation result, not a hardware demonstration. Its simulations report substantial reductions in qubit-round cost relative to conventional distillation approaches at representative physical error rates. The exact benefit depends on the code, noise model and target logical error rate.

The honest caveat

This is a component-level advance, not a full machine. It makes the big resource estimates more plausible but doesn't by itself build a CRQC — it's one of many pieces that all have to scale together.

Primary source: Gidney, Shutty & Jones, arXiv:2409.17595 (2024) ↗
2025Feb
Hardware · contested Context

Microsoft's Majorana programme — ambitious and still contested

Microsoft announced Majorana 1 in Feb 2025 and an upgraded Majorana 2 in Jun 2026, arguing that topological hardware could offer a scalable path to fault tolerance. The core topological interpretation remains actively contested in the scientific literature, so this belongs in the tracker as a high-upside but low-confidence hardware path.

Mechanism

A completely different bet from Google's and IBM's. Instead of correcting errors with software and extra qubits, a topological qubit would resist errors in the hardware itself — protection built into the physics. If it works, it needs far fewer physical qubits per logical one. It's the high-risk, high-reward path.

The numbers

Majorana 1 was announced as an eight-qubit device with a claimed architecture intended to scale much further. Microsoft announced Majorana 2 in June 2026, but device count and roadmap claims should be read separately from evidence that the underlying states are genuinely topological.

The honest caveat

Scientific disagreement is current, not historical. A Nature Matters Arising article published 24 Jun 2026 argued that transport data underlying the topological-gap interpretation may instead be consistent with trivial mechanisms; Microsoft published a formal reply defending its interpretation. Present the programme as contested evidence, not a settled topological-qubit milestone.

2029target
Roadmap Context

IBM "Starling" — fault-tolerant, dated

IBM's roadmap, still current in March 2026, targets Starling for 2029 with 200 logical qubits and 100 million gates. IBM's approach uses high-rate error-correcting codes and modular systems to reduce overhead, but the date remains a vendor roadmap goal rather than a demonstrated capability.

Mechanism

IBM is pursuing modular fault-tolerant systems based on high-rate quantum error-correcting codes rather than assuming surface-code overheads forever. If the planned code, coupler, decoder and module milestones work together, the architecture could substantially reduce the physical resources needed per useful logical computation.

The numbers

Current IBM roadmap: Kookaburra in 2026 to demonstrate a Starling module with logical processing plus quantum memory; Cockatoo in 2027 to demonstrate inter-module entanglement; Starling in 2029 targeting 200 logical qubits and 100 million gates.

The honest caveat

IBM explicitly labels the roadmap as current intent that is subject to change or withdrawal. Treat 2029 as a leading indicator of engineering ambition and investment — not as a forecast that Starling will necessarily ship on that date.

Primary source: IBM newsroom (June 2025) ↗
Decision framework · converting threat time into migration time

The Mosca Inequality — why migration can become urgent before Q-Day

The decision problem is not simply “When will a CRQC arrive?” It is whether the time needed to protect information exceeds the time available before that information could become vulnerable. Mosca’s framework makes those variables explicit without pretending that the CRQC date is known.

If X + Y > Z, the organisation is inside the risk window under the chosen threat horizon. The inequality does not prove Z; it shows why long-lived data and slow migration can require action even when the CRQC date remains uncertain.

The practical use is organisation-specific: estimate the confidentiality lifetime of important data, the realistic duration of cryptographic discovery and migration, and more than one plausible threat horizon. Sensitivity analysis is more defensible than treating a single survey midpoint as a deadline.

Why a future machine can create present exposure

Harvest Now, Decrypt Later (HNDL)

HNDL separates the attack into two moments: encrypted information can be collected before a CRQC exists, while quantum-enabled decryption occurs later. This is why confidentiality requirements can create migration pressure well before any public “Q-Day.”

Collection can occur before CRQC

US Executive Order 14412 explicitly recognises the risk that adversaries collect protected information now and decrypt it later when sufficiently capable quantum computers become available. The collection step is possible today; the future decryption step is the capability that remains uncertain.

The confidentiality deadline can precede Q-Day

If information must remain secret beyond the estimated time to CRQC, collection today can place that information inside an exposure window. The relevant calculation is therefore linked to data shelf-life, not only to the date on which a CRQC may appear.

Confidentiality and authentication have different risk profiles

HNDL most directly affects encrypted data whose confidentiality must survive into the future, making quantum-vulnerable key establishment a major migration concern. Digital-signature migration remains important as well, but its threat model is different because a future forgery cannot generally be “harvested” in the same way as ciphertext.

Hybrid designs require protocol-specific assurance

Combining classical and post-quantum mechanisms can reduce transition risk when the construction is standardised or otherwise well analysed for the protocol in question. Hybrid deployment is not a universal substitute for a planned PQC migration, and poorly designed combinations can introduce their own implementation and interoperability problems.

The regulatory clock · national & sector mandates

Dates are converging — but their legal force differs

Governments and security authorities have attached dates to PQC transition, but they are not all equivalent: some are laws or executive requirements for defined federal systems, some are procurement/security policies, and others are roadmaps or guidance. For planning, the relevant date is the earliest requirement or policy target that actually applies to the system, contract, sector or jurisdiction in question. Recommendations and draft plans should not be described as “binding” unless their legal or policy scope makes that true.

Featured national case study · Singapore

From quantum risk to migration practice

Singapore provides a useful case study of how a national programme can translate a global cryptographic risk into migration guidance, readiness assessment, network infrastructure, sector-specific action, and continuing technical review. The approach takes NIST post-quantum standards as a baseline, while treating QKD as a complementary option for niche high-assurance applications.

Why Singapore is featured

The significance is not that Singapore has declared a universal CRQC date or a single blanket migration deadline. It is that multiple public institutions are already turning uncertainty into practical preparation: guidance, readiness measurement, network capability, and sector-level expectations.

The counter-case

Reasons the CRQC timeline could still be longer than headline progress suggests

A credible tracker must preserve uncertainty. Resource estimates are not machines, logical scaling remains difficult, benchmark advantage does not equal cryptanalytic capability, and vendor roadmaps can slip. These points argue against panic and against treating any single year as “Q-Day”; they do not remove the need for multi-year migration planning.

Estimates are not machines

“Qubits to break RSA” figures are model-dependent resource estimates. They rely on specific assumptions about architecture, physical error, error correction, routing, runtime and classical control. No existing quantum computer can execute an RSA-2048 attack.

Logical scale remains an engineering challenge

Current logical-qubit demonstrations remain far below the scale and sustained operation required for cryptographically relevant algorithms. Scaling introduces system-level constraints including control, connectivity, decoding, calibration, leakage, correlated errors and modular integration.

Benchmark advantage is not cryptanalytic capability

Sampling and specialised benchmark results can demonstrate quantum computational advantage without implying the ability to run Shor-scale workloads. Classical algorithmic improvements can also narrow previously reported benchmark gaps.

Some high-profile claims remain contested or roadmap-dependent

Microsoft’s Majorana interpretation has faced published scientific scrutiny, and vendor roadmaps should be read as engineering targets rather than independent forecasts. A slower-than-expected path to fault tolerance remains plausible.

The counter-case mainly changes the plausible value of Z in the Mosca framework. It does not eliminate X (data shelf-life) or Y (migration time). This is why a risk-based migration programme can be justified without asserting an imminent CRQC.

How to read the tracker · methodology

Five evidence classes — deliberately kept separate

The credibility of a quantum-threat tracker depends less on collecting dramatic headlines than on identifying what kind of evidence each headline actually represents. A hardware demonstration, a resource estimate, an expert survey, a vendor roadmap and a government deadline answer different questions.

1 · Measured result

Demonstrated on real hardware or empirically observed. Strong evidence for what a system achieved under the reported conditions, but not automatically evidence of cryptanalytic capability.

2 · Resource estimate

Technical calculations under stated assumptions. Valuable for understanding feasibility, but sensitive to architecture, runtime, error model, code choice and engineering constraints.

3 · Expert opinion

Surveyed or attributed judgement about future timelines. Useful as a sentiment signal, but it remains opinion rather than measurement.

4 · Vendor roadmap / claim

Targets, engineering plans and company statements. Important signals of intent and investment, but not independent forecasts and not guaranteed delivery dates.

5 · Policy / standards

Standards, laws, executive orders, security policies, roadmaps and guidance. These can create real migration obligations or planning milestones even when the CRQC date remains uncertain.

The strongest conclusions appear when several independent evidence classes point in the same direction. Cross-paper qubit counts are not treated as directly comparable unless their assumptions, architecture and runtime are sufficiently aligned. Inference is labelled as inference, and contested claims are presented with the relevant scrutiny.

On the horizon · what would change the tracker

Leading indicators from August 2026 onward

The most useful signals are those that would alter the factual record, change confidence in the CRQC timeline, or change the urgency and sequencing of migration. These are the developments most likely to trigger an update.

Deployment signal · 12 Aug 2026

Google extends PQC into the Android device trust chain

Google announced Android 17 post-quantum security enhancements: ML-DSA integration in Verified Boot and Android Keystore, a transition of Remote Attestation toward PQC-compliant certificate chains, and hybrid signing support for apps. Pixel 11 launch coverage further reports that the new Titan M3 security chip adopts NIST PQC standards. The Titan M3-specific claim is currently supported by product reporting rather than an explicit Google hardware specification, so it should be treated as a deployment signal rather than independently verified hardware evidence. This increases implementation urgency slightly, but does not change the estimated CRQC arrival date or the resource-estimate charts.

Algorithms

Independent review of 2026 resource estimates

Watch whether neutral-atom Shor and P-256 ECDLP estimates survive independent scrutiny, implementation-level costing, alternative error models and reproduction.

Hardware

Logical-qubit scaling and sustained error performance

Watch for systems that combine larger logical-qubit counts with sustained logical error performance, useful circuit depth and credible paths to modular scaling.

Error correction

High-rate codes on real systems

qLDPC and other high-rate approaches may reduce fault-tolerance overhead under suitable architectures. Convincing system-level demonstrations would matter more than isolated theoretical ratios.

Standards

PQC moves from algorithms into protocols and infrastructure

Track IETF protocol work and the surrounding PKI, certificate, HSM, TPM and platform ecosystem that turns standardized algorithms into deployable infrastructure.

Policy

New migration requirements and implementation milestones

Changes in laws, executive orders, security policies, government guidance, procurement rules or sector requirements can alter migration urgency even without a hardware breakthrough.

Research thread

Implementation vulnerabilities after standardisation

A related research stream examines the gap between standardized PQC and safe deployment: validation, arithmetic and parameter handling, secret lifecycle, protocol integration, configuration and assurance boundaries.

Key terms

Glossary

CRQC
Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer — a quantum computer capable of breaking currently deployed public-key cryptography at a practically meaningful scale. No public system is known to meet this threshold today.
Q-Day
Informal shorthand for the future point at which quantum cryptanalysis becomes practically relevant. It is not a scientifically fixed date.
Physical vs. logical qubit
Physical qubits are hardware units subject to noise. Logical qubits encode protected quantum information using error correction and additional physical resources.
Below threshold
A regime in which increasing the size or distance of an error-correcting code reduces logical error. This is a key scaling condition for fault-tolerant quantum computing.
qLDPC codes
Quantum low-density parity-check codes — a family of quantum error-correcting codes with potentially higher encoding rates and lower overhead than surface-code approaches under suitable hardware and decoding assumptions.
Magic states
Special resource states used to implement non-Clifford operations required for universal fault-tolerant quantum computation. Producing them efficiently is an important resource-cost problem.
PQC
Post-Quantum Cryptography — classical cryptographic algorithms designed to resist known quantum attacks while running on conventional computing systems.
ML-KEM / ML-DSA / SLH-DSA
NIST FIPS 203 defines ML-KEM for key establishment; FIPS 204 and 205 define ML-DSA and SLH-DSA for digital signatures.
HNDL
Harvest Now, Decrypt Later — collecting encrypted information before a CRQC exists with the intention of decrypting it after quantum capability becomes sufficient.
Mosca framework
The relationship X + Y > Z, where data shelf-life plus migration time exceeds an assumed threat horizon. It is a risk-management framework, not a prediction of the CRQC date.
QKD
Quantum Key Distribution — key-distribution protocols whose security properties rely on quantum-mechanical behaviour and protocol assumptions. QKD is distinct from software-based PQC and is generally considered complementary rather than interchangeable.
Crypto-agility
The ability to discover, replace and manage cryptographic algorithms, protocols, certificates and keys without redesigning an entire system. It reduces the cost and risk of future cryptographic change.
