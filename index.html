Quantum risk · internal briefing

The code-breaking clock has moved forward.

Between 2022 and 2025 the expert-estimated chance of a cryptographically-relevant quantum computer within ten years rose sharply. At the same time, fault-tolerance experiments improved and published resource estimates for attacking RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography fell. The important signal is convergence across evidence types — not any single forecast or qubit number.

This page covers: the two charts, a two-front timeline of 14 developments, the Mosca decision framework, Harvest-Now-Decrypt-Later, the regulatory deadlines, a Singapore whole-of-government focus, the honest counter-case, a methodology/confidence note, a 2026 watch-list, sources, and a glossary.

The bigger story · resource-estimate compression

The target is moving — but the estimates are not like-for-like

The cleanest apples-to-apples result is Gidney's RSA-2048 estimate: about 20 million noisy qubits in 2019 to fewer than 1 million in 2025 under the same stated hardware assumptions. A separate 2026 neutral-atom study reaches cryptographically relevant Shor-scale workloads with as few as 10,000 reconfigurable atomic qubits, but uses a different architecture, error-correction design and runtime trade-off. Read these together as evidence that algorithms, error correction and hardware-aware co-design are all reducing the resources thought necessary for cryptanalysis.

Representative published physical-qubit resource estimates (log scale; not like-for-like)
Representative estimates: RSA-2048 20 million in 2019; RSA-2048 under 1 million in 2025 under the same stated assumptions; and a separate 2026 neutral-atom study with a roughly 10,000-qubit floor for cryptographically relevant Shor-scale workloads.

Do not read the three points as one continuous RSA curve. The 2019 and 2025 RSA estimates are directly comparable because Gidney states the same hardware assumptions. The 2026 Cain et al. result is architecture-specific neutral-atom/QEC co-design and trades space against much longer runtime. The defensible conclusion is resource compression across multiple technical fronts, not “RSA now needs 10,000 qubits.” Full sources below.

Why this matters alongside the survey: an annual opinion poll of 26 people is a sentiment indicator, while peer-reviewed experiments and resource analyses provide different evidence. Several important 2026 results appeared after the 2025 survey period, so they should be tracked separately rather than retroactively folded into the survey number.

Timeline

Why opinion moved — the events behind the numbers

The disruption since 2022 came from two independent directions at once, which is what makes it serious: the algorithms needed to break encryption got dramatically cheaper (Front 1), while the hardware, error-correction, and national programs all advanced (Front 2). Either alone would matter; together they compress the timeline from both ends. Click any event for the mechanism, the numbers, and the honest caveat.

◆ What the report itself attributes the shift to

To keep this honest: the 2025 Quantum Threat Timeline Report explicitly names the developments its experts cited as catalysts — Google's Willow processor, and two theoretical papers (Gidney, May 2025; Chevignard–Fouque–Schrottenloher, CRYPTO 2025). Those are marked “report-cited driver” below. But the survey is only a snapshot: it captures a much larger wave of algorithmic progress that began with Regev's 2023 breakthrough and continued after the survey closed with the 2026 neutral-atom and ECC results. Those are marked “foundational” or “theory” — real disruptions to the timeline that the 2025 survey number does not yet fully reflect. Events marked “context” (NIST, IBM, Microsoft) are part of the landscape but not named by the survey as causes. Every figure links to its primary source at the foot of the page.

2022
Baseline Survey

The quiet starting point

Most experts put under 30% on a 10-year timeline. The field was still about raw qubit count, not error correction — no fault-tolerance claims yet. This is the report first surfaced internally.

Mechanism

This is the "before" picture. Progress was measured in qubit count, and more qubits simply meant more errors — no one had shown you could scale a machine up without the noise scaling up with it. Everything that follows is the field solving exactly that problem.

The numbers

IBM's flagship that year was the 433-qubit Osprey. Expert consensus: well under 30% chance of a code-breaker within 10 years — and the authors note estimates had actually softened slightly from 2019–2021.

The honest caveat

Don't over-read the 2022 low point. The report's own authors attribute the 2022–2023 dip to a post-pandemic funding recalibration, not a genuine scientific slowdown — so treat this as a local floor, not the true trend.

Source: GRI Quantum Threat Timeline Report 2022 ↗

Front 1 · The algorithmic collapse — the target shrinks

These results reduce the qubits needed to break RSA/ECC. No new hardware — the goalposts move toward us. This is the thread the GRI survey most directly cited.

2023Aug
Theory Foundational

Regev breaks a 30-year deadlock on Shor's algorithm

Oded Regev published the first fundamental improvement to Shor's factoring algorithm in nearly three decades — a multidimensional variant needing far fewer quantum gates. This opened the algorithmic floodgates that Gidney and others poured through in 2024–2025.

Mechanism

Shor's 1994 algorithm was the benchmark for "how hard is it to break RSA" for 29 years. Regev found a genuinely new formulation — operating in a higher-dimensional space — that cuts the circuit size (gate count). Because every later resource estimate builds on the best available algorithm, a foundational improvement like this cascades into every downstream qubit count. It's the source of the collapse in the chart above.

The numbers

Reduces circuit size by a factor of ~√n for an n-bit integer, at the cost of ~√n separate runs. Follow-on work (Ragavan & Vaikuntanathan, Oct 2023) cut the space overhead back down, addressing its main drawback.

The honest caveat

Regev's algorithm relies on a heuristic number-theoretic assumption (since given a proof under certain conditions), and early implementations were actually slower than Shor's for small numbers. Its value is asymptotic and foundational, not an immediate practical speedup — but it reset the theoretical baseline.

Primary source: Regev, arXiv:2308.06572 (2023) ↗
2025Feb
Theory Report-cited driver

The 1,730-qubit result (Chevignard–Fouque–Schrottenloher)

A CRYPTO 2025 paper cut the number of logical qubits needed to factor RSA-2048 to just 1,730 — providing the algorithmic foundation that the Gidney estimate then compiled into physical hardware numbers. The 2025 report names this as a key theoretical advance its experts cited.

Mechanism

The threat timeline isn't only about building bigger machines — it's also about needing smaller ones. Every algorithmic improvement that reduces the qubits required to break RSA pulls Q-Day closer without a single new piece of hardware. This paper attacked the problem from the maths side, using approximate residue arithmetic to shrink the logical-qubit requirement dramatically.

The numbers

Reduced the logical-qubit count for factoring RSA-2048 to ~1,730 — roughly (0.5 + ε)·n logical qubits for an n-bit integer. This is the algorithmic groundwork the subsequent physical-resource estimate was built on.

The honest caveat

Logical qubits are not physical qubits — the earlier version of this algorithm needed an astronomically high gate count (~2×10¹² Toffoli gates), making it impractical until the follow-on work optimized it. It's a foundation, not a turnkey attack.

Primary source: Chevignard, Fouque & Schrottenloher, IACR ePrint 2024/1291 ↗
2025May
Theory Report-cited driver

Gidney: RSA-2048 with under 1 million qubits

Google researcher Craig Gidney's May 2025 paper cut the estimated physical qubits to break RSA-2048 from 20 million (his own 2019 figure) to under 1 million — a ~20× reduction, under identical hardware assumptions. This is arguably the single biggest input to the 2025 survey's jump, and the report names it directly.

Mechanism

This is the "the goalposts moved toward us" event. The threat was always framed as "we need a 20-million-qubit machine, and those are decades away." Gidney showed the same attack needs an order of magnitude fewer qubits — so the hardware the industry is already racing toward (IBM's roadmap, Google's Willow line) is much closer to the requirement than anyone assumed. Nothing new was built; the target simply got smaller.

The numbers

20 million qubits (Gidney–Ekerå 2019) → under 1 million (2025), roughly a 20× cut, with runtime under a week. Same stated assumptions both times: 0.1% gate error, 1 µs surface-code cycle, nearest-neighbour grid. The Toffoli-gate count was cut over 100× versus the 2024 approach it built on.

The honest caveat

This is a resource estimate, not a demonstration — no machine has factored RSA-2048. Gidney himself states he sees no way to shave another order of magnitude under the same assumptions, and endorses NIST's "deprecate after 2030, disallow after 2035" line rather than claiming imminent collapse.

Primary source: Gidney, arXiv:2505.15917 (2025) ↗
2026Mar
Theory Hardware-specific

Neutral-atom co-design pushes Shor-scale estimates toward ~10,000 qubits

A Caltech/Oratomic/UC Berkeley team (Cain, Preskill, Bluvstein et al.) reported that cryptographically relevant Shor-scale computation could be possible with as few as ~10,000 reconfigurable atomic qubits by co-designing algorithms, high-rate quantum error correction and neutral-atom hardware. This is a different architecture and runtime regime from Gidney's RSA estimate, so it should not be plotted as a direct continuation of the same curve.

Mechanism

This is where algorithm and hardware modality meet. qLDPC codes need long-range connections between qubits — something superconducting chips struggle with, but neutral atoms do naturally because they can be physically moved. Matching the code to the hardware slashes the requirement again. It also means watching only one hardware type (e.g. superconducting) gives a false sense of how far away the threat is.

The numbers

The paper's abstract gives an overall floor of about 10,000 reconfigurable atomic qubits for cryptographically relevant Shor-scale workloads. For P-256 it gives an example of about 26,000 physical qubits and a runtime of a few days; it says RSA-2048 would take one to two orders of magnitude longer. Those figures are architecture- and assumption-dependent.

The honest caveat

These are aggressive, assumption-heavy resource estimates with very long runtimes, and no neutral-atom machine is close to 10,000 error-corrected qubits today. But a slow code-breaker doesn't need to be fast — for "harvest now, decrypt later," patience is enough.

Primary source: Cain et al., arXiv:2603.28627 (2026) ↗
2026Mar
Theory Context

Google: P-256 ECDLP below 1,200 logical qubits in a 2026 resource study

Google researchers published new resource estimates for the 256-bit elliptic-curve discrete-log problem used in many modern TLS, SSH, code-signing and cryptocurrency deployments. Their study gives circuits below 1,200 logical qubits and models execution on a superconducting architecture using fewer than half a million physical qubits.

Mechanism

The GRI survey uses RSA-2048 as its benchmark, but many deployed systems rely on elliptic-curve public-key cryptography. Tracking ECDLP resource estimates therefore gives a complementary view of quantum risk; organisations should inventory the algorithms they actually use rather than infer their exposure from RSA alone.

The numbers

The 2026 whitepaper reports either <1,200 logical qubits with <90 million Toffoli gates, or <1,450 logical qubits with <70 million Toffoli gates. Under its model of a superconducting architecture with 10−3 physical error rates and planar connectivity, the circuits can run in minutes using fewer than 500,000 physical qubits.

The honest caveat

Still a resource estimate, not a demonstration. But it reframes the target: if your migration plan only tracks RSA timelines, it may be aimed at the slower of the two threats.

Primary source: Google Research / arXiv:2603.28846 (2026) ↗

Front 2 · The hardware & policy front — the machine gets closer

Real processors, error-correction milestones, national programs, and the standards/deadlines that turn all of it into an obligation.

2023Dec
Hardware pivot Context

From more qubits to better qubits

Investment described as skyrocketing. IBM ships its 1,121-qubit Condor but publicly pivots toward modular, error-corrected designs — conceding that raw scaling alone won't reach fault tolerance. 10-year survey estimate: 17–31%.

Mechanism

The tell was in one processor pair. IBM's giant 1,121-qubit Condor performed no better than the previous 433-qubit chip — proof that just adding qubits had hit diminishing returns. The smaller Heron, with far lower error rates, was the real signal: the industry pivoted from "biggest" to "cleanest."

The numbers

Condor: 1,121 qubits but roughly Osprey-level (433-qubit) performance. Heron: only 133 qubits, but error rates cut to about a third of the prior generation. 10-year survey estimate rose to 17–31%.

The honest caveat

Lower error rates on a small chip are not the same as error correction. Heron made qubits better; it did not yet make them self-correcting. That threshold wasn't crossed until Willow the following year. Note: this is landscape context — the survey does not name Condor as a driver.

Source: IBM Quantum roadmap ↗
2024Aug
Policy Context

NIST finalizes the PQC standards

FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), 204 (ML-DSA) and 205 (SLH-DSA) are published, giving organisations deployable NIST standards for key establishment and signatures. Standardisation continues for additional algorithms, but the migration problem is now operational rather than hypothetical. NSA's CNSA 2.0 schedule adds concrete milestones for National Security Systems.

Mechanism

This is the event that turns "quantum" from a research topic into a compliance obligation. Once NIST names the replacement algorithms and NSA attaches a deadline, migration stops being optional and starts being a scheduled program of work — regardless of exactly when a quantum computer arrives.

The numbers

Three standards were finalised on 13 Aug 2024: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA). HQC was selected in March 2025 as an additional KEM for future standardisation. NIST IR 8547 describes a staged transition away from quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography, but as of this fact-check it remains an Initial Public Draft, not a final binding rule.

The honest caveat

Standardizing the algorithms is the easy half. Real deployments have already hit implementation bugs — our own audit found a constant-time flaw in an SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) implementation — so "standard exists" is not "safely deployed." In the US, the Quantum Computing Cybersecurity Preparedness Act already requires federal agencies to inventory vulnerable systems and report migration progress. This is policy context, not a survey-cited driver.

Primary source: NIST FIPS 203/204/205, Federal Register (2024) ↗
2024Dec
Hardware Report-cited driver

Google Willow — below threshold

The first processor where adding qubits reduces the logical error rate. The 2025 report names Willow as a milestone its experts cited when revising estimates upward.

Mechanism

For thirty years, making a quantum computer bigger made it worse — errors piled up faster than you could fix them. Willow is the first machine where the curve bent the other way: scale the error-correcting code up, and the error rate goes down. That flip is the precondition for every large fault-tolerant machine. Without it, fault tolerance is theory; with it, it's engineering.

The numbers

105 qubits. Logical error ~0.14% per cycle, and a suppression factor Λ≈2.14 — the error roughly halves each time the code distance steps up. The distance-7 logical qubit outlived its best physical qubit by ~2.4× — the first demonstration of correction beating the raw hardware ("beyond break-even").

The honest caveat

Willow is still a research prototype in the noisy, pre-fault-tolerant era. It ran a contrived benchmark, not a useful algorithm, and is nowhere near breaking RSA. It proves the principle scales — it is not itself a threat.

Primary source: Acharya et al., Nature (2024) ↗
2025Mar
Hardware Geopolitical

China's Zuchongzhi 3.0 — the two-horse race is real

A USTC team (Pan Jianwei et al.) unveiled a 105-qubit superconducting processor claiming quantum advantage on random-circuit sampling far beyond classical reach. The 2025 GRI report itself flags China as the most likely challenger within five years — and notes zero survey respondents are based there.

Mechanism

The threat timeline isn't only technical — it's geopolitical. A cryptographically relevant machine built in secret by a state actor wouldn't be announced; the report explicitly warns covert programs could put the true timeline 2+ years ahead of open publications. Visible parity between US and Chinese hardware is a proxy for how fast the invisible race may be moving.

The numbers

105 qubits, 182 couplers. Gate fidelities: 99.90% single-qubit, 99.62% two-qubit (PRL 134, 090601). Claimed a random-circuit-sampling task that would take the Frontier supercomputer ~6.4 billion years. Note: this is a sampling benchmark, not a code-breaking demonstration.

The honest caveat

Random-circuit sampling is a contrived benchmark with no cryptographic relevance, and classical algorithms sometimes claw back the "advantage" later. This shows national capability and pace, not proximity to breaking RSA.

Primary source: Phys. Rev. Lett. 134, 090601 (2025) ↗
2025Nov
Hardware Different modality

Quantinuum Helios — trapped ions hit 48 logical qubits

Quantinuum's 98-physical-qubit trapped-ion Helios system reported 99.921% two-qubit gate fidelity and demonstrations including 48 error-corrected logical qubits at a 2:1 physical-to-logical encoding rate. A reminder that fault-tolerance progress must be tracked across multiple hardware modalities, not only superconducting systems.

Mechanism

Helios uses trapped ions with high connectivity and high physical-gate fidelity, and Quantinuum demonstrated high-rate logical encodings using concatenated codes. The 2:1 result is a specific code-and-workload demonstration; it should not be read as a universal physical-to-logical conversion ratio for arbitrary fault-tolerant computation.

The numbers

Quantinuum reports 98 physical qubits at 99.921% two-qubit gate fidelity and 99.9975% one-qubit gate fidelity. Helios demonstrations include 94 error-detected logical qubits in a GHZ state, 50 error-detected logical qubits in a magnetism simulation, and 48 error-corrected logical qubits with better-than-physical performance.

The honest caveat

48 logical qubits is worlds away from the thousands needed for RSA, and trapped-ion clock speeds are slower than superconducting. High fidelity and low overhead are real advantages, but scale remains the open question for every platform.

Source: Quantinuum roadmap & Helios ↗
2024Sep
Error correction Overhead cut

Magic state cultivation — a proposed route to lower fault-tolerant overhead

Gidney, Shutty & Jones proposed “magic state cultivation,” a protocol designed to produce the non-Clifford resource states needed for universal fault-tolerant computation with physical-gate costs comparable to a lattice-surgery CNOT of similar reliability. It targets one of the expensive components in fault-tolerant resource budgets.

Mechanism

Error-corrected machines can do "easy" (Clifford) gates cheaply, but universal computation needs "magic states," historically made by distillation — enormous factories consuming huge numbers of physical qubits. Cultivation grows them directly inside a logical qubit, potentially eliminating whole distillation rounds. Every resource estimate above (Gidney's <1M, the neutral-atom 10K) quietly depends on advances like this to be credible.

The numbers

The cited 2024 paper is a protocol-and-simulation result, not a hardware demonstration. Its simulations report substantial reductions in qubit-round cost relative to conventional distillation approaches at representative physical error rates. The exact benefit depends on the code, noise model and target logical error rate.

The honest caveat

This is a component-level advance, not a full machine. It makes the big resource estimates more plausible but doesn't by itself build a CRQC — it's one of many pieces that all have to scale together.

Primary source: Gidney, Shutty & Jones, arXiv:2409.17595 (2024) ↗
2025Feb
Hardware · contested Context

Microsoft's Majorana programme — ambitious and still contested

Microsoft announced Majorana 1 in Feb 2025 and an upgraded Majorana 2 in Jun 2026, arguing that topological hardware could offer a scalable path to fault tolerance. The core topological interpretation remains actively contested in the scientific literature, so this belongs in the tracker as a high-upside but low-confidence hardware path.

Mechanism

A completely different bet from Google's and IBM's. Instead of correcting errors with software and extra qubits, a topological qubit would resist errors in the hardware itself — protection built into the physics. If it works, it needs far fewer physical qubits per logical one. It's the high-risk, high-reward path.

The numbers

Majorana 1 was announced as an eight-qubit device with a claimed architecture intended to scale much further. Microsoft announced Majorana 2 in June 2026, but device count and roadmap claims should be read separately from evidence that the underlying states are genuinely topological.

The honest caveat

Scientific disagreement is current, not historical. A Nature Matters Arising article published 24 Jun 2026 argued that transport data underlying the topological-gap interpretation may instead be consistent with trivial mechanisms; Microsoft published a formal reply defending its interpretation. Present the programme as contested evidence, not a settled topological-qubit milestone.

Independent scrutiny: Nature Matters Arising (Jun 2026) ↗ Microsoft reply ↗
2029target
Roadmap Context

IBM "Starling" — fault-tolerant, dated

IBM's roadmap, still current in March 2026, targets Starling for 2029 with 200 logical qubits and 100 million gates. IBM's approach uses high-rate error-correcting codes and modular systems to reduce overhead, but the date remains a vendor roadmap goal rather than a demonstrated capability.

Mechanism

IBM is pursuing modular fault-tolerant systems based on high-rate quantum error-correcting codes rather than assuming surface-code overheads forever. If the planned code, coupler, decoder and module milestones work together, the architecture could substantially reduce the physical resources needed per useful logical computation.

The numbers

Current IBM roadmap: Kookaburra in 2026 to demonstrate a Starling module with logical processing plus quantum memory; Cockatoo in 2027 to demonstrate inter-module entanglement; Starling in 2029 targeting 200 logical qubits and 100 million gates.

The honest caveat

IBM explicitly labels the roadmap as current intent that is subject to change or withdrawal. Treat 2029 as a leading indicator of engineering ambition and investment — not as a forecast that Starling will necessarily ship on that date.

Primary source: IBM newsroom (June 2025) ↗
What it means for us · the decision framework

The Mosca Inequality — turning a timeline into a deadline

The threat isn't a date on a calendar; it's a relationship. Michele Mosca's inequality is the standard tool for converting "how far away is quantum" into "are we already exposed." If the time to protect our data is longer than the time until it can be broken, we're too late — and the arithmetic below is what every board should see.

If X + Y > Z, the organisation has entered the risk window. The 2025 survey puts a CRQC within ten years in a 28–49% probability range, while official migration roadmaps such as the UK's extend to 2035. That does not prove a universal deadline; it means each organisation should quantify its own data shelf-life (X), realistic migration time (Y), and conservative threat horizon (Z) now.

Applied to us: the action item isn't "wait for Q-Day." It's to estimate our own X (how long does our most sensitive data need to stay confidential?) and Y (how long would a full cryptographic inventory and migration realistically take?), and compare the sum to a conservative Z. For most organisations with long-lived secrets, X + Y already exceeds any plausible Z — which is the entire argument for starting now.

Why a future machine is a present threat

Harvest Now, Decrypt Later (HNDL)

The most important idea for leadership to internalise: an adversary does not need a quantum computer today to attack us today. They need only to record our encrypted traffic now and store it until a CRQC exists.

The attack is already possible

The risk is recognised explicitly in US Executive Order 14412: ongoing cyber activity creates the possibility that adversaries collect information now and decrypt it later once large-scale quantum computers are operational. The collection can occur before a CRQC exists; only the decryption capability is deferred.

Our real deadline is earlier than Q-Day

For any data with a 10–15 year confidentiality requirement, the effective deadline is Q-Day minus the shelf-life. If a CRQC arrives in 2033 and our data must stay secret for 12 years, data stolen today is already compromised. The clock ran out before it started ticking.

Signatures vs. secrets behave differently

HNDL threatens confidentiality (encrypted data), so key-exchange migration (ML-KEM) is the urgent priority. Digital signatures are less exposed to harvesting — a forged signature is only useful after a CRQC exists — but still need migration before Q-Day.

Hybrid buys time safely

Deploying classical + post-quantum together (hybrid mode) protects against HNDL now without betting everything on a young algorithm. It's the recommended transitional posture in most national roadmaps.

The regulatory clock · national & sector mandates

Dates are converging — but their legal force differs

Governments and security authorities have attached dates to PQC transition, but they are not all equivalent: some are laws or executive requirements for defined federal systems, some are procurement/security policies, and others are roadmaps or guidance. For planning, use the earliest requirement or policy target that actually applies to your systems, contracts and jurisdictions — and do not label a recommendation “binding” unless its scope makes that true.

The honest counter-case

Reasons the timeline might be longer than it looks

Intellectual integrity requires steelmanning the other side. A briefing that only argues for acceleration invites the obvious pushback — so here it is, made as strongly as the optimistic case. These caveats don't reverse the conclusion (the Mosca arithmetic still says act now), but they're why "act now" means "plan and migrate," not "panic."

Estimates are not machines

Every "qubits to break RSA" figure is a resource estimate under idealised assumptions (fixed error rates, specific architectures). No quantum computer has factored anything larger than small demonstration numbers. The gap between a paper's assumptions and a working fault-tolerant machine is enormous — and historically underestimated.

Logical ≠ physical, and scale is brutal

Today's best machines have tens of logical qubits (Quantinuum's 48); RSA needs thousands of high-quality logical qubits sustained over billions of operations. Each order of magnitude in scale has historically taken years and surfaced new engineering problems (crosstalk, wiring, cryogenics, decoder latency).

Benchmarks can be clawed back

"Quantum advantage" claims (Sycamore, Zuchongzhi) have repeatedly been narrowed or matched by improved classical algorithms afterwards. Random-circuit-sampling speed is not cryptographic capability, and headline "×10¹⁵ faster" figures don't translate to breaking encryption.

Contested results and hype cycles

Microsoft's Majorana claim drew serious scientific dispute. Vendor roadmaps are marketing as much as engineering, and the field has a history of optimistic dates slipping. Some respected physicists still put fault tolerance decades out.

Why this doesn't change the recommendation: HNDL means we're defending against the earliest plausible Z, not the median. Even if the skeptics are right and a CRQC is 20 years away, data we need secret for 15 years and a migration that takes 8 still fails the Mosca test. The counter-case lengthens Z; it doesn't shrink X + Y.

How to read this page · methodology

Three kinds of evidence, not one

Everything on this page falls into one of three tiers of certainty. Keeping them separate is what makes the analysis trustworthy — and prevents an opinion figure from being cited as if it were a measurement.

Measured

Demonstrated on real hardware and published in peer-reviewed venues: Willow's below-threshold error correction, Quantinuum's 48 logical qubits, Zuchongzhi's fidelities. The most solid tier — but none of it is yet a code-breaker.

Estimated

Resource calculations under stated assumptions: e.g. 20M→<1M for RSA under comparable 2019/2025 assumptions, plus separate 2026 neutral-atom and ECC studies. These are assumption-dependent; cross-paper figures are often not like-for-like. Compare architecture, runtime and error model before comparing qubit counts.

Opinion

The GRI survey: 26 experts' subjective probabilities. Valuable as sentiment, but a lagging indicator with a small pool, wide bins, and the authors' own caveats. Never cite the 28–49% without the sample-size context.

The strongest claims here combine tiers: a measured hardware trend, plus an estimated resource collapse, plus rising expert opinion, all pointing the same direction. Where only one tier supports a claim (e.g. a single vendor roadmap date), it's flagged as such. Causation between events and the survey's movement is limited to what the report itself states — inference is labelled as inference.

On the horizon · what to watch next

Leading indicators from August 2026 onward

Because opinion surveys lag, these are the concrete signals that would move the timeline before the next annual report captures them. Tracking these is how we stay ahead rather than reacting.

Algorithm

Independent review of 2026 resource estimates

Watch whether the new neutral-atom Shor and P-256 ECDLP estimates survive independent scrutiny, implementation-level costing and alternative error models. Reproduction matters more than another headline minimum.

Hardware

First logical-qubit scaling milestones

IBM Loon/Kookaburra, Quantinuum's path to Apollo, QuEra's Megaquop system. Watch for logical-qubit counts crossing into the low hundreds with sustained error rates.

Error correction

qLDPC on real hardware

The ~90%-overhead-cut codes are mostly on paper. A convincing hardware demonstration of high-rate qLDPC would materially pull in fault-tolerance timelines.

Geopolitics

Covert-program signals

The GRI report warns state programs could be 2+ years ahead of open publications. National funding announcements and classified-lab hints are worth monitoring.

Standards

PQC in protocols

IETF finalising PQC for TLS (~2027), plus HSM/TPM and X.509 certificate standards. These turn algorithms into deployable infrastructure.

Our research

Implementation vulnerabilities

Standardised ≠ safely deployed. Our own SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) constant-time finding shows the migration surface has its own bugs — a thread worth expanding as libraries mature.

Key terms

Glossary

CRQC
Cryptographically-Relevant Quantum Computer — one powerful enough to break RSA-2048 (or equivalent) in a practical timeframe. None exists yet.
Q-Day
The (future) day a CRQC first exists. The thing every timeline is trying to estimate.
Physical vs. logical qubit
Physical qubits are the noisy hardware units; many are combined via error correction into one stable logical qubit. RSA needs thousands of logical qubits.
Below threshold
The point where adding more physical qubits reduces the logical error rate instead of raising it — the precondition for scaling (Willow, 2024).
qLDPC codes
Quantum low-density parity-check codes — error-correcting codes needing far fewer physical qubits per logical qubit than surface codes (~90% less overhead in IBM's plan).
Magic states
Special states required for universal computation; historically made by costly "distillation," now cheaper via "cultivation."
PQC
Post-Quantum Cryptography — classical algorithms believed secure against quantum attack. The NIST standards below are the approved set.
ML-KEM / ML-DSA / SLH-DSA
The finalised NIST standards: FIPS 203 (key exchange), 204 and 205 (signatures). Replacements for RSA and ECC.
HNDL
Harvest Now, Decrypt Later — recording encrypted data today to decrypt once a CRQC exists.
Mosca Inequality
X + Y > Z: if data shelf-life plus migration time exceeds time-to-CRQC, you're already exposed.
QKD
Quantum Key Distribution — a hardware method of exchanging keys whose security rests on physics, not maths. Distinct from PQC (which is software/algorithmic). Singapore's MAS actively pilots both; most Western roadmaps favour PQC as the primary path.
Crypto-agility
Designing systems so cryptographic algorithms can be swapped without re-architecting — the capability MAS and most regulators emphasise as the first practical step.
