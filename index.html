Why ~2033?

When could the cryptographic breakthrough happen?

A cryptographically relevant quantum computer would break the RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography protecting almost everything online. Here is where the evidence points — read as the overlap of two independent trajectories, not a prediction.

Current assessment

The quantum threat is uncertain in timing. The migration problem is not.

No public result shows that a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists today. But standards are final, migration takes years, Harvest-Now-Decrypt-Later creates exposure before CRQC arrival, and deployment signals are now visible across major platforms and enterprise infrastructure.

2 Jun 2026 · VERIFIED SOURCE · HARDWARE / VENDOR ROADMAP · CONTESTED INTERPRETATION

Microsoft Majorana 2 adds a distinct topological route to the hardware trajectory

Microsoft reports that Majorana 2 replaces the earlier InAs–Al stack with InAs–Pb devices, with mean parity lifetimes of about 20 seconds, microsecond-scale operations and more than a 1,000× lifetime improvement over Majorana 1. Microsoft consequently moved its target for a scalable practical quantum computer to 2029. The programme is decision-relevant because a hardware-protected topological qubit could change fault-tolerance scaling if validated, but the underlying topological interpretation remains actively disputed in the scientific literature. This broadens the capability-side evidence; it does not establish cryptanalytic capability or move the ~2033 synthesis on its own.

8 Sep 2026 · VERIFIED · RESOURCE ESTIMATE

IonQ publishes an end-to-end secp256k1 attack-resource estimate

IonQ published a fully compiled, architecture-specific fault-tolerant estimate for solving the secp256k1 elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem: 19,397 physical qubits, 1,457 logical qubits and approximately 39 million logical Toffoli operations, with an estimated 25.7-day runtime per attempt. The study is a resource estimate, not a demonstrated attack, and IonQ states that no machine capable of executing it exists today. It materially strengthens the evidence that modeled cryptanalytic resource requirements are falling, but does not establish a convergence date or justify moving the tracker's ~2033 assessment on its own.

IonQ announcementTechnical paper
2–8 Sep 2026 · VERIFIED · HARDWARE / QUANTUM ERROR CORRECTION

Quantinuum Helix experimentally validates a compact fault-tolerant architecture

Quantinuum reports experimental validation of its C4-Helix [[20,2,6]] fault-tolerant architecture on the 98-qubit Helios trapped-ion processor. The reported experiments cover repeated error correction, logical computation and logical entanglement between different QEC codes, with encoded implementations outperforming corresponding unencoded physical baselines without postselection. This is measured fault-tolerance evidence on real hardware, not a cryptanalytic demonstration, and it strengthens the quantum-computing capability side of the convergence model without establishing a new Q-Day date.

15 Jul 2026 · VERIFIED · RESOURCE ESTIMATE / PREPRINT

Luo et al. reduce 256-bit ECDLP logical width to 835 qubits

A space-efficient Shor/ECDLP construction reports an 835-logical-qubit requirement for a 256-bit prime-field elliptic-curve discrete logarithm, below prior logical-width estimates cited by the authors. This is an algorithm-level resource estimate rather than a physical-machine blueprint, so it complements rather than replaces end-to-end estimates such as IonQ's architecture-specific secp256k1 study.

TLS PQ key establishment broadens; authentication standardisation advances while deployment still lags

RFC 10024 standardises interoperable hybrid ML-KEM + ECDHE groups for TLS 1.3. Following IESG approval on 3 Sep, the direct ML-KEM specification progressed through IANA processing on 10 Sep, with MLKEM512, MLKEM768 and MLKEM1024 assigned TLS Supported Groups values 512–514; the document is approved for publication and awaiting final RFC publication. It remains a work in progress rather than a published RFC, while production dependencies across certificates, cryptographic modules, protocol implementations and operational infrastructure remain material.

Algorithmic and logical-resource reductions
VERIFIEDRESOURCE ESTIMATE

Physical-machine estimates are only one part of the resource story. Algorithmic work can reduce the logical width or circuit cost before any hardware architecture is chosen. Examples include Regev's 2023 factoring formulation, the Chevignard–Fouque–Schrottenloher reduced-qubit RSA result, and Luo et al.'s 2026 space-efficient 256-bit ECDLP construction requiring 835 logical qubits under the paper's model. On 14 Sep 2026, Jo and Lee reported a further asymptotic ECDLP reduction to 5n/2 + o(n) logical qubits with Õ(n²) Toffoli gates, improving the leading logical-qubit term and gate complexity relative to Luo et al. The authors explicitly leave concrete cryptographic-size estimates open, so 5n/2 must not be presented as a complete P-256 circuit count. These results show that the attack requirement itself continues to move.

Architecture Convergence Observatory · question: can each hardware modality supply the required computation?

Different quantum-computing architectures are closing the cryptanalytic gap in different ways

This section tracks the hardware side of convergence by modality: can a particular quantum-computing architecture realistically supply the computation required by serious cryptanalytic estimates? The security question is not which architecture will “win”, but whether any credible architecture can close its own gap. Each modality is therefore assessed using its architecture-specific resource estimate, current capability evidence, remaining gap and confidence — without treating physical qubits as interchangeable units.

Fresh observation. Current serious estimates are no longer concentrated in a single hardware modality. High-attention today: superconducting, trapped ion, neutral atom and bosonic cat qubits, because each has either a serious target-specific physical-resource estimate or a credible within-architecture compression result. Topological / Majorana is now tracked as a distinct high-upside, higher-uncertainty modality because Microsoft reports Majorana 2 and a 2029 scalable-system target, while independent scientific scrutiny of the topological interpretation remains active. Distributed silicon spin remains a lower-confidence watch because its current QRE is vendor-authored. Photonic FTQC is progressing, but this audit did not identify a modern target-specific physical-resource point strong enough to plot beside the others.
Assessment effect. Architecture diversity strengthens the case for an architecture-neutral threat model, but it does not justify a new Q-Day date. The central ~2033 risk window remains unchanged.

Theory Foundational

Regev breaks a 30-year deadlock on Shor's algorithm

Oded Regev published the first fundamental improvement to Shor's factoring algorithm in nearly three decades — a multidimensional variant needing far fewer quantum gates. Subsequent work explored how the approach and related arithmetic improvements could affect concrete quantum resource estimates.

Why it matters
Mechanism

Shor's 1994 algorithm was the benchmark for "how hard is it to break RSA" for 29 years. Regev found a genuinely new formulation — operating in a higher-dimensional space — that cuts the circuit size (gate count). Because every later resource estimate builds on the best available algorithm, a foundational improvement like this cascades into every downstream qubit count. It's the source of the collapse in the chart above.

The numbers

Reduces circuit size by a factor of ~√n for an n-bit integer, at the cost of ~√n separate runs. Follow-on work (Ragavan & Vaikuntanathan, Oct 2023) cut the space overhead back down, addressing its main drawback.

The honest caveat

Regev's algorithm relies on a heuristic number-theoretic assumption (since given a proof under certain conditions), and early implementations were actually slower than Shor's for small numbers. Its value is asymptotic and foundational, not an immediate practical speedup — but it reset the theoretical baseline.

Error correction Overhead cut

Magic state cultivation — a proposed route to lower fault-tolerant overhead

Gidney, Shutty & Jones proposed “magic state cultivation,” a protocol designed to produce the non-Clifford resource states needed for universal fault-tolerant computation with physical-gate costs comparable to a lattice-surgery CNOT of similar reliability. It targets one of the expensive components in fault-tolerant resource budgets.

Why it matters
Mechanism

Error-corrected machines can do "easy" (Clifford) gates cheaply, but universal computation needs "magic states," historically made by distillation — enormous factories consuming huge numbers of physical qubits. Cultivation grows them directly inside a logical qubit, potentially eliminating whole distillation rounds. Every resource estimate above (Gidney's <1M, the neutral-atom 10K) quietly depends on advances like this to be credible.

The numbers

The cited 2024 paper is a protocol-and-simulation result, not a hardware demonstration. Its simulations report substantial reductions in qubit-round cost relative to conventional distillation approaches at representative physical error rates. The exact benefit depends on the code, noise model and target logical error rate.

The honest caveat

This is a component-level advance, not a full machine. It makes the big resource estimates more plausible but doesn't by itself build a CRQC — it's one of many pieces that all have to scale together.

Google reported below-threshold surface-code quantum memories on its Willow processors: logical error decreased as code distance increased, a key condition for scalable quantum error correction.

Why it matters
Mechanism

Below-threshold operation means increasing the size of the error-correcting code suppresses logical error rather than amplifying it. That behaviour is foundational for large fault-tolerant systems, but it is still several engineering steps removed from cryptanalytic computation.

The numbers

On the 105-qubit Willow processor, the distance-7 surface-code memory used 101 qubits and reported a logical error of 0.143% per cycle with an error-suppression factor Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02. The logical memory lifetime exceeded its best constituent physical qubit by about 2.4×.

This was an error-corrected memory experiment, not an RSA or ECC attack and not a general-purpose fault-tolerant computer. It demonstrates an important scaling property, while large-scale computation still requires much larger systems, logical operations, decoding, stability and system integration.

Theory Resource-estimate context

The 1,730-qubit result (Chevignard–Fouque–Schrottenloher)

A CRYPTO 2025 paper estimated that factoring RSA-2048 can be carried out using about 1,730 logical qubits, trading qubit count against a much larger number of operations and repeated runs. Gidney’s 2025 physical-resource estimate explicitly cites this approximate-residue arithmetic as one of the main sources of its qubit-count reduction.

Why it matters
Mechanism

The threat timeline isn't only about building bigger machines — it's also about needing smaller ones. Every algorithmic improvement that reduces the qubits required to break RSA pulls Q-Day closer without a single new piece of hardware. This paper attacked the problem from the maths side, using approximate residue arithmetic to shrink the logical-qubit requirement dramatically.

The numbers

Reduced the logical-qubit count for factoring RSA-2048 to ~1,730 — roughly (0.5 + ε)·n logical qubits for an n-bit integer. This is the algorithmic groundwork the subsequent physical-resource estimate was built on.

The honest caveat

Logical qubits are not physical qubits — the earlier version of this algorithm needed an astronomically high gate count (~2×10¹² Toffoli gates), making it impractical until the follow-on work optimized it. It's a foundation, not a turnkey attack.

Mechanism

The Mosca Inequality — why migration can become urgent before Q-Day

The decision problem is not simply “When will a CRQC arrive?” It is whether the time needed to protect information exceeds the time available before that information could become vulnerable. Mosca’s framework makes those variables explicit without pretending that the CRQC date is known.

What to take away

Core idea: Migration can become urgent before a CRQC exists.

Why: Long-lived data and multi-year migration consume the available preparation window.

Decision implication: Cryptographic discovery and migration lead time matter now.

Why a future machine can create present exposure

Harvest Now, Decrypt Later (HNDL)

HNDL separates the attack into two moments: encrypted information can be collected before a CRQC exists, while quantum-enabled decryption occurs later. This is why confidentiality requirements can create migration pressure well before any public “Q-Day.”

Collection can occur before CRQC

US Executive Order 14412 explicitly recognises the risk that adversaries collect protected information now and decrypt it later when sufficiently capable quantum computers become available. The collection step is possible today; the future decryption step is the capability that remains uncertain.

EvidenceEO 14412NIST HNDL explainer

The confidentiality deadline can precede Q-Day

If information must remain secret beyond the estimated time to CRQC, collection today can place that information inside an exposure window. The relevant calculation is therefore linked to data shelf-life, not only to the date on which a CRQC may appear.

EvidenceGRI Mosca frameworkNIST HNDL explainer

Confidentiality and authentication have different risk profiles

HNDL most directly affects encrypted data whose confidentiality must survive into the future, making quantum-vulnerable key establishment a major migration concern. Digital-signature migration remains important as well, but its threat model is different because a future forgery cannot generally be “harvested” in the same way as ciphertext.

EvidenceGoogle migration timelineNIST PQC

Hybrid designs require protocol-specific assurance

Combining classical and post-quantum mechanisms can reduce transition risk when the construction is standardised or otherwise well analysed for the protocol in question. Hybrid deployment is not a universal substitute for a planned PQC migration, and poorly designed combinations can introduce their own implementation and interoperability problems.

Data lifecycle · where quantum exposure appears

Where quantum risk enters the data lifecycle

At rest, in transit and in process are useful places to look, but the quantum risk follows the cryptography rather than the storage state itself. Prioritisation should combine the public-key dependency, how long protection must last, and how difficult that dependency will be to replace.

Three data states. Different exposures. Different migration decisions.

A quantum-safe programme should not simply label every dataset “quantum vulnerable”. The useful question is where quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography is used to establish confidentiality, authenticate systems, sign software or protect keys — and whether the protected information or system will still matter when a CRQC becomes plausible.

The distinction to remember

Confidentiality can be retrospective: captured encrypted information may retain value long enough to be decrypted later.

Implementation risk is present-tense: libraries, PKI, applications, appliances and HSM integrations may take years to discover, change and validate.

At rest

How long must this remain confidential?

Databases · files · archives · backups · object stores · long-lived records
Assessment question: Which data assets require confidentiality beyond the expected migration period, and what quantum-vulnerable cryptographic dependencies protect them?

What to note

  • Identify sensitive datasets by confidentiality lifetime, retention period and impact of future disclosure.
  • Map RSA/ECC dependencies across key establishment, key wrapping, envelope encryption, PKI, certificates, HSM/KMS integrations, archival and recovery workflows.
  • Distinguish quantum-vulnerable public-key protection from symmetric encryption. PQC migration does not imply replacing every cryptographic primitive.
  • Prioritise data whose required secrecy lifetime may extend beyond the time needed to discover, test and migrate its cryptographic dependencies.
  • Include backups, replicas and archives. A production system may be migrated while historical copies remain dependent on legacy cryptography.

Action

  • Map data shelf-life to the cryptography and keys that protect it.
  • Prioritise assets whose confidentiality requirement extends furthest into the plausible quantum-threat window.

In transit

Can this be captured now and decrypted later?

TLS · VPN · SSH · APIs · messaging · service-to-service links · external connections
Assessment question: Which communication paths use quantum-vulnerable key establishment and carry information whose confidentiality must survive future cryptanalytic capability?

What to note

  • Map external and internal data flows and identify RSA/ECC-dependent TLS, VPN, SSH, messaging and machine-to-machine connections.
  • Assess exposure to Harvest-Now-Decrypt-Later based on data sensitivity, confidentiality lifetime and the feasibility of capturing encrypted traffic.
  • Record protocol versions, cipher suites, certificates, key-establishment mechanisms, gateways, proxies, load balancers and termination points.
  • Identify dependencies where PQC support must interoperate across clients, servers, network appliances, cloud services and third parties.
  • Test migration effects including handshake size, latency, fragmentation, interoperability and middlebox behaviour rather than assuming algorithm substitution is transparent.

Action

  • Prioritise high-value flows with long confidentiality lifetimes.
  • Test PQC-capable or hybrid protocol options, interoperability, performance and certificate/PKI impacts before production rollout.

In process / in use

Where does quantum-vulnerable cryptography affect processing, trust and control?

Applications · cryptographic libraries · HSM/KMS · PKI · code signing · identity · authentication · software/firmware verification
Assessment question: Which applications, protocols and trust mechanisms depend on RSA/ECC, and what must change to replace those dependencies without breaking security or operations?

What to note

  • Discover RSA/ECC usage in applications, libraries, APIs, protocols, certificates, HSM/KMS interfaces, authentication systems and embedded components.
  • Separate confidentiality/key-establishment dependencies from digital-signature and authentication dependencies. They have different migration paths and risk drivers.
  • Include PKI, certificate issuance and validation, code signing, firmware signing, secure boot, identity and machine/service authentication.
  • Determine whether cryptography is configurable, library-controlled, hardware-bound, protocol-bound or embedded in third-party products.
  • Assess crypto-agility: algorithm replacement, parameter changes and certificate/key transitions should be possible without unacceptable disruption.
  • Test the complete implementation path. Algorithm availability alone does not establish interoperability, operational safety or correct PQC integration.
  • NIST's June 2026 PIV working drafts illustrate the engineering depth of migration: proposed ML-DSA and ML-KEM support adds PQC key references, certificate containers and data objects under a dual-stack transition approach. NIST labels these as preliminary working materials rather than formal public drafts.

Action

  • Build ownership into the cryptographic inventory: system, library, key/certificate, vendor, upgrade path and migration dependency.
  • Validate implementation correctness, interoperability and operational behaviour, not just algorithm support.

The regulatory clock · national & sector mandates

Dates are converging — but their legal force differs

Governments and security authorities have attached dates to PQC transition, but they are not all equivalent: some are laws or executive requirements for defined federal systems, some are procurement/security policies, and others are roadmaps or guidance. For planning, the relevant date is the earliest requirement or policy target that actually applies to the system, contract, sector or jurisdiction in question. Recommendations and draft plans should not be described as “binding” unless their legal or policy scope makes that true.

What to take away

Direction: Transition dates are becoming operational planning constraints.

Caution: Legal force and scope differ by jurisdiction and sector.

Decision implication: Map each deadline to the systems and obligations it actually covers.

The counter-case

Reasons the CRQC timeline could still be longer than headline progress suggests

A credible tracker must preserve uncertainty. Resource estimates are not machines, logical scaling remains difficult, benchmark advantage does not equal cryptanalytic capability, and vendor roadmaps can slip. These points argue against panic and against treating any single year as “Q-Day”; they do not remove the need for multi-year migration planning.

What to take away

What could slow the threat: Fault-tolerant scaling may remain harder than resource studies imply.

What does not disappear: Data shelf-life and migration lead time still exist.

Decision implication: Uncertainty can change timing without removing the case for preparation.

Estimates are not machines

“Qubits to break cryptography” figures are model-dependent. IonQ's 19,397-physical-qubit secp256k1 result is unusually complete because it maps through a specific QEC/hardware architecture, but IonQ itself states that no machine capable of executing the attack exists today. Logical scale remains an engineering challenge

Cryptanalytic workloads still require orders-of-magnitude more sustained logical computation plus control, decoding, routing and system integration.

Sampling and specialised benchmark results can demonstrate quantum computational advantage without implying the ability to run Shor-scale workloads. Classical algorithmic improvements can also narrow previously reported benchmark gaps.

How to read the tracker · methodology

Five evidence classes — deliberately kept separate

The credibility of a quantum-threat tracker depends less on collecting dramatic headlines than on identifying what kind of evidence each headline actually represents. A hardware demonstration, a resource estimate, an expert survey, a vendor roadmap and a government deadline answer different questions.

Architecture-neutral threat assessment

The tracker does not assume which quantum-computing modality will ultimately reach cryptographically relevant scale. It monitors measured progress and credible resource estimates across superconducting, trapped-ion, neutral-atom, bosonic cat-qubit, distributed silicon-spin and photonic approaches, while retaining logical-level algorithmic results separately. Architecture-specific physical-qubit counts are not treated as directly comparable unless the cryptographic target, runtime, QEC and hardware assumptions are sufficiently compatible.

Key terms

Glossary

CRQC
Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer — a quantum computer capable of breaking currently deployed public-key cryptography at a practically meaningful scale. No public system is known to meet this threshold today.
Definition/sourceNIST PQC explainer
Q-Day
Informal shorthand for the future point at which quantum cryptanalysis becomes practically relevant. It is not a scientifically fixed date.
Definition/sourceGRI threat timeline
Physical vs. logical qubit
Physical qubits are hardware units subject to noise. Logical qubits encode protected quantum information using error correction and additional physical resources.
Definition/sourceWillow — NatureIBM Starling explainer
Below threshold
A regime in which increasing the size or distance of an error-correcting code reduces logical error. This is a key scaling condition for fault-tolerant quantum computing.
Definition/sourceWillow — Nature
qLDPC codes
Quantum low-density parity-check codes — a family of quantum error-correcting codes with potentially higher encoding rates and lower overhead than surface-code approaches under suitable hardware and decoding assumptions.
Definition/sourceCain et al. 2026IBM FTQC roadmap
Magic states
Special resource states used to implement non-Clifford operations required for universal fault-tolerant quantum computation. Producing them efficiently is an important resource-cost problem.
Definition/sourceMagic state cultivation
Topological / Majorana qubit
A proposed qubit architecture that seeks to encode quantum information in non-local topological degrees of freedom so that some errors are suppressed by the hardware physics itself. Microsoft is pursuing this approach, but whether its current devices establish the claimed topological regime remains scientifically contested.
Definition/contextMicrosoft Research roadmapNature scrutiny
PQC
Post-Quantum Cryptography — classical cryptographic algorithms designed to resist known quantum attacks while running on conventional computing systems.
Definition/sourceNIST PQC
ML-KEM / ML-DSA / SLH-DSA
NIST FIPS 203 defines ML-KEM for key establishment; FIPS 204 and 205 define ML-DSA and SLH-DSA for digital signatures.
Definition/sourceNIST PQC standards
HNDL
Harvest Now, Decrypt Later — collecting encrypted information before a CRQC exists with the intention of decrypting it after quantum capability becomes sufficient.
Definition/sourceEO 14412NIST explainer
Mosca framework
The relationship X + Y > Z, where data shelf-life plus migration time exceeds an assumed threat horizon. It is a risk-management framework, not a prediction of the CRQC date.
Definition/sourceGRI/evolutionQ 2019
QKD
Quantum Key Distribution — key-distribution protocols whose security properties rely on quantum-mechanical behaviour and protocol assumptions. QKD is distinct from software-based PQC and is generally considered complementary rather than interchangeable.
Definition/sourceIMDA NQSN+CSA COS 2026
Crypto-agility
The ability to discover, replace and manage cryptographic algorithms, protocols, certificates and keys without redesigning an entire system. It reduces the cost and risk of future cryptographic change.
Definition/sourceNIST CSWP 39upd1
